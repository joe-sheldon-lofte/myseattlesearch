### **EP1**

January settles over the northern shore of Lake Washington with a quiet, heavy mist that hangs low in the Douglas fir canopy. Back in 1912, real estate developer and future Seattle Mayor Ole Hanson laid out this community with a strict founding charter that forbade the unwitting cutting of trees. That early mandate shaped the town into a densely forested slope where roads wind along natural hill contours rather than straight grid lines. Today, the municipal code still protects any tree larger than six inches in diameter, giving the start of the year an atmosphere defined by dripping greenery and crisp, quiet air.

When the post-holiday reset demands a quiet morning outing, head straight to the Town Center for a hot espresso at Third Place Books. After browsing the towering wooden shelves for a winter read, step outside and take the brief five-minute stroll south across Bothell Way NE down to the pier at Lyon Creek Waterfront Park. Sitting on the end of the wooden deck as cold fog floats across the dark water offers an immediate, zero-dollar mental reset.

Daily routines here require a clear strategy for navigating the terrain. With zero heavy commercial development allowed outside the main shopping complex on Bothell Way NE, almost every errand leads back to the local QFC or requires a quick trip west to the commercial strip along Highway 99. When the evening commute backs up at the intersection where SR 522 meets SR 104, long-time residents bypass the idling line of brake lights by slipping up Perkins Way NE or cutting across NE 178th Street to reach the upper hill neighborhoods.

Long before the historic Red Brick Road was paved through here in 1914, the ancestral lands of the Snohomish and Duwamish peoples anchored the northern rim of the lake. Winter was traditionally a time for gathering in shoreline settlements along these salmon streams, a rhythm that still resonates in the quiet season. As dusk falls before five o'clock, the neighborhood retreats into its wooded hillsides, trading the cold lake air for cozy living rooms wrapped in deep green timber.

### **EP2**

Mid-winter rains turn the forest floor soft and thick, filling the stream beds of McAleer and Lyon Creeks with steady runoff. In 1914, workers laid the famous Red Brick Road along Bothell Way, creating the first paved highway link between Seattle and the timber towns to the north. That historical ribbon of brick established the area as a vital gateway, yet the neighborhood managed to protect its residential core by outlawing commercial strip malls everywhere except the central valley hub.

On a cold, rainy afternoon, escape the downpour by spending two easy hours inside Third Place Commons at the Town Center. Grab a warm cup from a local spot nearby like Radiant Flavors Cafe or Morning Glory Chai, then settle into the public hall to watch a live acoustic lunch performance or challenge a neighbor to a game on the public chess boards. It is a completely free, warm spot where you can sit as long as you like without anyone pressuring you to order another latte.

Everyday life in February involves dodging puddles and mastering local parking quirks. Weekend morning visitors often clog the lower lot near the retail storefronts, but locals know to park on the spacious upper deck garage near the north entrance for seamless access. Living here without a personal car means putting up with steep hillside walks and limited bus routes, so keeping a reliable vehicle tuned up is just part of the bargain for living inside a forest canopy.

February brings a particular comfort to those who appreciate low-key local routines and historical grounding. The Coast Salish heritage of the lake shore reminds us that these waterways have provided shelter from winter squalls for centuries. As rain taps against skylights and tree limbs, the town feels isolated in the best possible way, offering a calm pace where you can slow down and let the winter days unfold without hurry.

That spirit of civic preservation is woven into the neighborhood identity, where residents treat public parks as extensions of their own backyards.

Take advantage of a dry March window by planning a brief morning outing starting at Pfingst Animal Acres. Walk the soft, wooded paths along McAleer Creek to admire the early green shoots and community garden beds, then hike the gentle ridge trail upward toward Horizon View Park. Standing at the high-elevation benches at Horizon View gives you a broad view across the treetop canopy just as the early spring light breaks through shifting rain clouds.

Spring sports and weekend errands make timing everything on the local roadways. If you are aiming for a mid-day run on the Burke-Gilman Trail, get out there before nine in the morning to beat the influx of regional cyclists who clog the shoreline path on sunny weekend afternoons. For quick meals between errands, locals skip the highway crowds and grab fresh tacos from Taqueria El Cerrito or hit up the spot down at Lake Forest Park Fishnchips Spotlight for a quick bite.

### **EP4**

April arrives with cherry blossoms along the residential streets and daylight stretching past seven thirty in the evening. This green, quiet setting was home to world-renowned science fiction author Octavia E. Butler, who lived and wrote here from 1999 until her passing in 2006. Her seminal novel Fledgling took shape under this very tree canopy, proving that the thick woods and tranquil atmosphere have long nourished creative, independent minds.

For a peaceful two-hour afternoon loop, head to the Grace Cole Nature Area. Walk the wooden boardwalks that wind through fifteen acres of protected wetland sanctuary, listening to the chorus of spring frogs and spotting emerging native plants among the dense cedar forest. After exploring the wetlands, make the quick drive down to the Town Center patio for a hot coffee and a pastry from Backyard Bakery to enjoy in the late afternoon sun.

Living in a heavily wooded hillside town requires managing unique practical realities when spring arrives. Pollen coats windshields, and power lines occasionally battle with overhanging hemlock branches during high winds. Because residential streets are notoriously narrow and winding by original 1912 design, driving carefully around sharp, unlit curves is second nature to locals who share the road with morning dog walkers and kids heading to bus stops.

As April brightens the lake shore, the neighborhood shakes off the heavy darkness of late winter. The Coast Salish history of fishing these pristine tributary streams reminds residents of the delicate ecosystem right in their side yards. Watching the evening light linger over the water brings a fresh sense of momentum to daily walks, turning simple trips to the mailbox into moments of appreciation for the forest canopy.

Evening twilight now stretches toward nine o'clock, inviting long walks after dinner through the neighborhood loops.
