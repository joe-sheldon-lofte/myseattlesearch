### **EP1**

January in Lynnwood arrives with low fog clinging to the wet firs, smoothing out the sharp edges of our fast-evolving concrete grid. A century ago, back when the Puget Mill Company platted five-acre plots for the "Little Landers" during the 1920s Alderwood Manor egg boom, hundreds of thousands of laying hens dominated these hills long before developers poured the first asphalt on 196th Street SW. Today, as high-density towers rise under 350-foot regional height limits at the City Center transit hub, that agricultural grit still lingers underneath the suburban layout. Long before the chicken coops or real estate developer Karl O'Beirn named the crossroads after his wife Lynn in 1937, the indigenous Snohomish and Tulalip peoples moved through these forested uplands, following seasonal rhythms that respect the quiet winter chill.

When the post-holiday reset demands a calm morning, duck into Rise & Shine Bakery on Highway 99 for warm baguettes and sweet egg tarts that steam up the glass display. Take your paper bag and head ten minutes east into Scriber Lake Park, where a quiet 1.2-mile floating boardwalk loop weaves right across the open water and sleeping marshland. Benches along the path offer front-row seats to waterfowl navigating the mist, far removed from the surrounding commercial hum. It is a two-hour low-key ritual that resets your lungs and reminds you how much green space thrives right beneath the retail surface.

Daily errands this time of year require a bit of strategic maneuvering along the arterial corridors. If you are aiming for the morning commute from Lynnwood City Center Station, you need your bumper in the garage before 7:15 AM, or you will end up pivoting to the overflow Ride-Store lots along 44th Avenue W. When 196th Street SW locks up between Interstates and Highway 99 during the dark evening rush, local drivers skip the mess entirely by cutting across 200th Street SW or 188th Street SW.
