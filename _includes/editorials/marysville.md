### **EP1**

Back in 1872 when Civil War veteran James P. Comeford set up his modest trading post on the banks of Ebey Slough, January meant huddling against the river fog before logging timber claims. That same damp chill still hangs over Ebey Slough today, softening the edges of the city's old shingle mill past into a quiet winter sanctuary. Downtown retains its human scale along 3rd Street, where strict height limits keep the skyline open to the cold Cascades and the looming shadow of Mt. Pilchuck.

Start a clear January morning at Caffe Dell'Amore on State Avenue, where you can grab a properly pulled espresso in a cozy nook before the day gets moving. From there, take the four-block stroll down to Ebey Waterfront Park to walk the flat, paved shoreline trail along Ebey Slough. Low tide brings out bald eagles and blue herons over the tidal mudflats, giving you a front-row view of the Snohomish River Delta without a single jet ski in sight.

Navigating town on a gray winter weekday means respecting the stark divide between State Avenue and the historic core. State Avenue is the functional commercial spine where you handle vehicular errands, but afternoon bottlenecks near the freeway can turn simple trips into a headache. If northbound I-5 starts backing up at Exit 199, locals skip the gridlock entirely by peeling off early at Marine View Drive in Everett or using the 67th Ave NE north-south arterial corridor to slide north quietly.

January gives this community room to breathe right alongside its tribal neighbors on the Tulalip Reservation. This land has been shaped for centuries by the Snohomish and allied Coast Salish peoples who signed the 1855 Point Elliott Treaty, and that long history feels close when the winter river mist rolls in. You do not need a grand plan to appreciate the town this month, just a warm jacket, a quiet bench at the waterfront, and the simple comfort of slow-paced mornings.

### **EP2**

February in the Pacific Northwest is an endurance test of gray skies, but this town has always known how to hunker down. Long before punk drummer Patty Schemel was banging out early grunge rhythms in local garages here with her band Sybil before joining Hole, Marysville was defined by tough cedar mills and hard-scrabble timber workers. That unpretentious, roll-up-your-sleeves character remains deeply embedded in the local DNA today.

When the February drizzle feels endless, head west just past town limits to the Hibulb Cultural Center on the Tulalip Reservation. Inside the 23,000-square-foot facility, you can spend two hours immersed in Lushootseed language history, Coast Salish canoe carving traditions, and historic tribal artifacts. After soaking in that deep regional story, head back across the boundary to State Avenue for a steaming plate of carne asada burritos at Tacos Guaymas or authentic street tacos at Taqueria El Rey to warm up from the inside out.

Midwinter errands require a tactical approach to local streets, especially along the central strip. Finding parking in the historic core along 3rd Street is surprisingly painless with free two-hour angled spots right in front of local shops. If you need to cut across to the east side toward Snohomish or Lake Stevens without getting trapped at every single red light on State Avenue, cut east early on 88th St NE straight over to Highway 9.

There is a quiet solidarity in the way locals navigate the late-winter gloom together. Whether you are retreating into the quiet reading alcoves at the Sno-Isle Marysville Public Library on 61st St NE or catching a game at Home Plate Pub, people are content to keep things simple. The rain hits the tin roofs, Ebey Slough runs high and dark, and everyone counts down the weeks until spring teases its way back into the valley.

### **EP3**

March arrives with sudden sun squalls and high-velocity cloud gaps over the Snohomish Delta. Before it became famous as the Strawberry City in the 1920s with over two thousand acres of fruit fields, this valley was pure wetland timberland cleared by pioneer saws. When early spring light hits the 1921 historic water tower standing eighty feet high in Comeford Park, you get a sense of how quickly this pioneer stop transformed into an agricultural powerhouse.

Fuel up for a March afternoon by ordering a slow-smoked brisket sandwich at Jeff's Texas Style BBQ on State Avenue, where the central Texas pitmaster tradition delivers serious comfort. Take your meal over to Jennings Nature Park, claim a covered picnic bench near Allen Creek, and set off on the three-point-four mile Jennings Loop walk. The trail winds past the Master Gardener demonstration plots and the historic 1888 Gehl House log cabin, where preserved cedar logs show exactly what homesteading looked like in late nineteenth-century Snohomish County.

Spring home improvement runs mean local traffic starts picking up heavily around commercial hubs. If you are heading out toward the outlet malls or Quil Ceda Boulevard on a March weekend, timing is everything. Arriving before half past ten in the morning guarantees a spot near the main walkways, or you can park on the outer perimeter lot near Quil Ceda Boulevard for an effortless escape when the crowds swell.

As daylight stretches out, the town begins shaking off its winter slumber along the greening banks of Allen Creek. The nearby Bernie Kai-Kai Gobin Salmon Hatchery continues its vital work sustaining Chinook and Coho populations for the Snohomish River watershed, serving as a reminder of how closely this land is tied to the water. A quick walk through Deering Wild Flower Acres on 84th St NE offers a quiet, low-stimulus sensory sanctuary under a dense canopy of native hemlock and fir as early wildflowers break through the mud.

### **EP4**

April brings an unmistakable shift as daylight stretches past seven-thirty and cherry blossoms burst into bloom around Comeford Park. The town was platted in 1885 by James Comeford, but its agricultural identity took off when local soil proved remarkably fertile for berries and orchards. That dirt-under-the-fingernails farming legacy still lingers in the air every spring when local gardens wake up across the residential neighborhoods.

Start your April outing with an acai bowl and a specialty espresso from Spoon & Straw on State Avenue, taking advantage of the emerging patio weather. Walk two blocks south into Comeford Park to stroll under the flowering trees and inspect the base of the historic concrete water tower. Finish your loop by heading down to the Ebey Waterfront Park boardwalk, where spring bird migration brings thousands of waterfowl sweeping through the estuary against the distant backdrop of the Olympic Mountains.

Driving through town on sunny April afternoons calls for knowing how to dodge the heavy commute choke-points. State Avenue carries the brunt of north-south local traffic, and signal timing can test your patience during peak hours. Using 67th Ave NE as your primary north-south bypass saves time and stress whether you are running errands in Smokey Point or sliding back into downtown.

Springtime highlights the rich multicultural texture of the city's main commercial strip. Small independent spots like Tindahan Sa Nayon offer Filipino specialty groceries, sitting alongside family-owned Mexican bakeries and Asian noodle shops that keep the corridor fed. The atmosphere is open and welcoming, filled with neighbors greeting each other on sidewalk patios as winter jackets are finally left at home.

### **EP5**

May introduces those long Pacific Northwest twilights where the sun refuses to set until almost nightfall. By 1910, ten sawmills were running full steam along Ebey Slough, sending cedar shingles out across Puget Sound and shaping the town's work ethic. Today, that industrial waterfront has transformed into a peaceful public parkway, proving how working landscapes can evolve while honoring their history.

Spend a May evening grabbing an artisanal espresso at Caffe Dell'Amore before driving east to the Jennings Nature Park arboretum. The smooth, paved paths make for an easy, ADA-accessible loop suitable for wheelchairs or strollers as you wander among native plant exhibits. Sit on a view bench along Allen Creek to watch red-winged blackbirds dart through the cattails, taking in the serene green space as the light turns soft over the trees.

As warmer weather brings more visitors through Snohomish County, I-5 corridor delays become a regular feature of late afternoon drives. When northbound traffic locks up around Exit 199, savvy commuters take Marine View Drive out of Everett to bridge the slough smoothly. Parking at Jennings Park is rarely an issue, featuring ample no-cost civic spaces that make spur-of-the-moment evening walks completely hassle-free.

The town radiates a relaxed pre-summer energy during May. Local volunteers can be seen tending the WSU Master Gardener demonstration plots in Jennings Park, sharing advice on composting and vegetable cultivation with interested passersby. Kids ride bikes down quiet residential side streets, and the sense of community connection feels tangible as neighbors spend more time out in their yards.

### **EP6**

June belongs entirely to the strawberry, a tradition rooted in the 1920s when thousands of acres of crimson fruit covered the surrounding fields. The annual Strawberry Festival began in 1932 as a way to celebrate farm families during tough economic times, and it remains the ultimate civic ritual today. When the Grand Parade rolls down State Avenue, the city's historic identity as a tight-knit agricultural valley comes alive in full color.

Kick off a June morning by picking up a legendary Dutch maple bar or orange bread from Oosterwyk's Dutch Bakery on 3rd Street, a seventy-year-old local institution operating in a historic 1920s building. Walk down to Ebey Waterfront Park with your treats to watch early morning kayakers launch from the public dock at high tide. Stroll along the flat boardwalk, watching the morning fog burn off the water as the Cascades appear in the eastern sky.

Parade week in mid-June requires significant navigation planning, as major sections of State Avenue close down for floats and marching bands. To avoid getting stuck in event traffic, use 67th Ave NE for north-south travel and stick to 88th St NE for east-west routes. Downtown 3rd Street offers free two-hour angled parking, but on festival weekend, parking earlier in the morning at Ebey Waterfront Park is your best bet for walking access.

Solstice daylight means lingering outside until well past nine in the evening. Families line the sidewalks for open-air concerts at the Comeford Park amphitheater, eating strawberry shortcake while kids run around the historic 1921 water tower. The spirit of Indigenous economic sovereignty championed by the late Tulalip Tribal leader John McCoy shines through during these summer gatherings, highlighting a shared respect for land, water, and community history.

### **EP7**

July brings golden sunshine and clean saltwater breezes drifting inland from Port Susan and the Puget Sound. Long before paved roads connected these towns, old US Highway 99 served as a crucial macadam road bridging Everett and Arlington through Marysville's timber heart. Today, summer traffic moves much faster, but the town's function as a welcoming crossroads remains unchanged.

Cool down on an eighty-degree July afternoon by heading to The Creamery on State Avenue for classic scoops or a thick milkshake. Take your ice cream down to the Ebey Slough Overlook at Ebey Waterfront Park and claim a shaded public bench. From this zero-dollar vantage point, you can watch tidal currents sweep through the wetlands while taking in sweeping views of Mount Rainier rising over the southern horizon.

Summer weekend traffic around the Tulalip retail centers and Seattle Premium Outlets can turn Quil Ceda Boulevard into a slow crawl. If you are hitting local stores for summer gear, beat the rush by shopping before eleven in the morning or using perimeter access roads to skip main intersection bottlenecks. For daily grocery runs, State Avenue offers convenient drive-in access, while historic 3rd Street remains a calm pedestrian zone.

July nights are custom-made for sitting outside at local spots like Whitewall Brewing Company on Smokey Point Boulevard. The pioneer craft brewery features a dog-friendly taproom where local food trucks park out front and neighbors gather over cold draft beers. The atmosphere is warm and casual, filled with people recapping high-tide kayak trips down the slough or weekend outings to nearby Puget Sound beaches.

### **EP8**

August hangs heavy and warm over the Snohomish River Delta, dusting the mountain view corridors along 3rd Street in soft golden light. Strict town height limits guarantee that even on hazy late-summer afternoons, the silhouette of Mt. Pilchuck dominates the eastern horizon just as it did when pioneer logging crews first cleared these woods.

Spend a late August afternoon grabbing custom boba teas or iced coffee from Dreamy Drinks on State Avenue. Drive over to Deering Wild Flower Acres on 84th St NE for a quiet, shaded walk along six acres of passive nature trails under towering fir trees. Finish your outing by heading west to the Ebey Waterfront Park boardwalk just before dusk to watch the sun drop behind the Olympic Mountains in a blaze of orange and purple.

Late summer brings heavy recreational travel along I-5, leading to frequent delays near Exit 199 on Friday afternoons. Locals know to avoid the freeway altogether when running errands between downtown and Smokey Point, relying on the 67th Ave NE corridor instead. If you are heading out to Lake Stevens or Snohomish, cutting east on 88th St NE straight to SR 9 remains the fastest route around town congestion.

August evenings have a lazy, magical rhythm where nobody feels like rushing home. Families gather on the lawns at Comeford Park for free outdoor summer concerts while kids play around the gazebo. Over at Quil Ceda Village, traditional alder-wood salmon roasts honor Coast Salish culinary traditions, filling the evening air with rich woodsmoke and deep ties to the surrounding waters.

### **EP9**

September brings a sharp autumn clarity to the air as morning fog lingers low over Ebey Slough before burning off into warm, sunny afternoons. This transition month reflects the town's historical shift from dense timber country into an agricultural center where seasonal harvests dictated daily life. That seasonal pulse still dictates local life today as schools reopen and harvest stands set out their fall crops.

Start a September morning with a fresh coffee and a specialty treat from Spoon & Straw on State Avenue before setting out for a peaceful walk along the Jennings Nature Park loop. Stroll past the Master Gardener demonstration gardens to inspect late-season vegetables and blooming autumn flowers. The paths are quiet on autumn weekdays, offering a serene outdoor retreat right in the heart of town.

Back-to-school traffic alters morning driving patterns across the city, particularly near major school zones along 67th Ave NE and State Avenue. Plan extra time for morning commutes between seven-thirty and eight-thirty, or use secondary side streets to bypass school drop-off queues. Parking at civic sites like Comeford Park or the Sno-Isle Marysville Public Library remains simple and free all month long.

There is a satisfying crispness to September evenings as the sun sets a little earlier over the delta mudflats. Local sports fans gather at Home Plate Pub on State Avenue to catch autumn football games over draft beers, while birdwatchers return to the Ebey Waterfront Park pier to spot migrating flocks overhead. The neighborhood feels grounded, cozy, and ready for the cozy season ahead.

### **EP10**

October wraps the city in rich autumn color as maples and cottonwoods turn brilliant shades of yellow and deep red along Allen Creek. In the historic 1920s Marysville Bakery building on 3rd Street, decades of baking history continue to warm up chilly October mornings. The building once stood as the tallest structure in town, a testament to 3rd Street's historic role as the civic heart of the community.

Stop by Oosterwyk's Dutch Bakery on 3rd Street for a warm apple fritter or a loaf of their famous orange bread, still made using classic recipes and vintage baking equipment. Take your treats on a fall foliage stroll through Deering Wild Flower Acres, where rain-dampened earth smells like native fir and fallen leaves. The quiet six-acre nature preserve offers complete solitude away from commercial traffic, making it an ideal autumn escape.

Autumn rains bring slick roads and early darkness, making visibility along unlit sections of State Avenue a priority for local drivers. Downtown angled parking on 3rd Street is ideal for quick, dry hops into local shops without dealing with large mall parking lots. If you need indoor sports recreation on rainy days, Volli indoor pickleball arena on State Avenue provides an active indoor alternative without needing to worry about the weather.

As dusk falls around six o'clock, the town settles into a warm, atmospheric calm. Low clouds hang over the Cascades, framing the mountain view corridors along downtown streets in dramatic shades of slate and blue. Whether you are grabbing a family dinner at Cristiano's Pizza Etc or picking up takeout tacos, people embrace the autumn turn with genuine warmth and local pride.

### **EP11**

November arrives with strong winter storms that push high tides up Ebey Slough, rattling tin roofs and reminding everyone of the river's raw power. Back in 1910, these heavy autumn rains meant logging crews had to secure timber rafts against churning river currents. That wild, water-driven history still feels alive when November gales blow inland from Puget Sound, turning the town into a cozy refuge.

Escape a rainy November afternoon by placing an order for slow-smoked Texas brisket and house sausage at Jeff's Texas Style BBQ on State Avenue. Head over to the Sno-Isle Marysville Public Library on 61st St NE to find a comfortable reading alcove near large window views of rainy trees. Spend two quiet hours reading, eating, and watching storm clouds track across the sky in complete indoor comfort.

Darkness settling in by four-thirty in the afternoon means peak rush hour happens in full darkness under heavy rain. When northbound I-5 turns into a sea of red taillights around Exit 199, avoid the frustration by exiting early at Marine View Drive in Everett or taking 67th Ave NE north toward Smokey Point. Parking lots at local parks like Jennings or Ebey Waterfront are virtually empty on rainy days, offering easy pull-in spots for quick storm watching from your driver's seat.

November brings people together inside welcoming community hubs. Families gather for hot bowls of Northwest clam chowder and wild cod fish and chips at Ivar's Seafood Bar on State Avenue, while inclusive community programs at the public library keep kids and adults engaged out of the cold. The town embraces its cozy side, finding comfort in good food, warm hearths, and familiar neighborhood faces.

### **EP12**

December covers the city in festive holiday lights, transforming the historic 1921 water tower in Comeford Park into a shining beacon for the neighborhood. First constructed over a century ago as a vital piece of pioneer infrastructure, the eighty-foot concrete landmark now stands as the emotional center of winter celebrations, grounding the community in its long history.

Start a December evening by grabbing a hot espresso or specialty drink from Caffe Dell'Amore on State Avenue. Drive down to 3rd Street to browse independent shops for local holiday gifts, taking advantage of free two-hour street parking right along the storefronts. Finish your night with a slow walk through Comeford Park to see the illuminated historic water tower and gazebo shining against the crisp winter night sky.

Holiday shopping at Seattle Premium Outlets and Tulalip retail centers brings high traffic volumes to Quil Ceda Boulevard throughout December. To avoid getting stuck in commercial gridlock, local shoppers stick to downtown 3rd Street or handle errands early in the morning before ten-thirty. When crossing east toward Highway 9, 88th St NE remains the fastest bypass around local traffic spots.

The month ends with a deep sense of community warmth and shared traditions. Families gather for hot Italian comfort food and gourmet pizzas at Cristiano's Pizza Etc, while local craft beer enthusiasts raise holiday toasts at Whitewall Brewing Company. As winter darkness settles over Ebey Slough, the town remains grounded, friendly, and proudly rooted in its unique Pacific Northwest identity.
