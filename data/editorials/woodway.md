### **EP1**

Back in 1912, a Seattle attorney named David Whitcomb Sr. bought up 320 acres of dense timberland to create an exclusive countryside retreat he called Woodway Park. He wanted an escape from the soot and noise of the growing city, establishing a secluded residential sanctuary under a thick evergreen canopy. Today, that quiet ethos remains completely intact across the entire town footprint. There are zero streetlights, no concrete sidewalks, and not a single corner market or gas station inside the municipal line.

A winter morning here calls for a slow two-hour routine that begins with a misty walk along the gravel footpaths of Woodway Reserve on 114th Ave W. The five-acre preserve is wrapped in dripping Douglas fir branches and lush sword ferns that smell like deep forest floor after a hard freeze. After completing the loop, drive two minutes across the northern border into the Edmonds Bowl to grab a hot pour-over and a fresh pastry at Grounded Espresso on 4th Ave N.

Living in a town with zero commercial zoning means your daily routines require a bit of intent. You are not stepping out your front door to buy a carton of milk or a loaf of bread. Every errand run takes you 1.5 miles east to the QFC at Westgate on Edmonds Way or down into Shoreline. Public street parking is strictly banned along residential roads that lack paved shoulders, so if you are visiting the local trails, you must park in the designated gravel lot at Woodway Reserve or near Town Hall.

Long before timber developers carved out two-acre parcels, Snohomish and Suquamish Lushootseed-speaking peoples utilized these coastal bluffs and local waterways for seasonal marine transit and gathering. That deep quiet still defines January days when low fog settles over Puget Sound. The complete lack of streetlamps makes winter nights profoundly dark, offering a peaceful sensory retreat where the only sound is winter rain hitting the heavy fir canopy.

### **EP2**

On February 26, 1958, local land owners officially incorporated Woodway as a legal defense mechanism to block annexation attempts by neighboring Edmonds and to permanently protect their strict two-acre minimum lot sizes. They even designed the historic Town Hall at 23920 113th Place W to look like a simple residential home so it would blend seamlessly into the dense woods. Officially classified as a Code City, civic leadership still intentionally retains the traditional title Town of Woodway.

When gray February skies roll in, clear your schedule for a restorative outing down Algonquin Road toward the coastal edge. Take a quiet, rain-dampened stroll beneath the looming trees while listening to the wind come off the water, then drive straight to The Churchkey Pub on 4th Ave N for elevated comfort food in a cozy basement setting.

Navigating the local roads in winter comes with specific local wisdom. When sudden squalls or heavy overcast days make coastal bluffs too raw, residents pivot indoors to the shelter of the Edmonds Library or a corner seat at Walnut Street Coffee. If you are commuting during peak afternoon hours when state route 104 backs up with ferry traffic heading to the Kingston terminal, cut south along 100th Ave W through the eastern boundary to reach Richmond Beach Road without idling in line.

February highlights the deep winter lull that defines life in this timber-lined enclave. Walking along the paved road shoulders without streetlights forces you to slow down and tune into the natural rhythm of the season. The absence of commercial neon or storefront glare preserves a rare, uninterrupted darkness that feels remarkably distant from the surrounding Puget Sound suburbs.
