### **EP1**

Long before high-tech offices filled the region, this valley was a rough-and-tumble logging and coal hub known as Gilman. When Isaac Brock of Modest Mouse wandered these damp streets in his youth, he captured that raw, melancholic edge that still lingers over the valley when winter settles in. The state legislature officially renamed the settlement Issaquah back in 1899, but that old frontier DNA remains embedded in the street grid beneath the mountain peaks.

On a misty January morning, start your day with a warm cup from Issaquah Coffeeroasters in Olde Town. Step out onto the paved Rainier Trail and follow it north as the low fog hangs over the valley floor. The trail brings you right into Confluence Park, where Issaquah Creek runs dark and cold under the footbridges, offering a peaceful, quiet vantage point before the town fully wakes up.

Getting around town this time of year requires a bit of strategy. Front Street South locks up tight with commuter traffic between six and nine in the morning as drivers pour in from southern King County. Local residents skip that bottleneck entirely by cutting across 2nd Ave SE or taking Sunset Way directly to the freeway on-ramps. For big grocery runs at Target or Costco, heading down NW Gilman Blvd early in the morning keeps you out of the gridlock.

The valley carries a deep human history that reaches back far beyond the early railroad days. The Snoqualmie Indian Tribe and Duwamish peoples named this place after the calls of the water birds along the creek. If you need a quiet retreat from the winter chill, take a slow walk along the flat Tradition Plateau Swamp Trail on Tiger Mountain, where the thick forest canopy keeps the rain at bay and the local wildlife stays active.

### **EP2**

Winter in the valley has a way of turning everyone inward toward warm lights and heavy wooden doors. Back in 1956, a Swiss-born alpine adventurer named Julius Boehm looked at the dark slope of Tiger Mountain and saw a piece of his homeland. He built Boehm's Candies on East Sunset Way, constructing an authentic European chalet and Swiss Chapel that still stand today as a sweet monument to alpine persistence.

For a classic two-hour winter escape, grab a cozy booth for lunch at Montalcino Ristorante Italiano along Front Street. After finishing your meal, wander past the historic Memorial Field gazebo and follow the creek path south toward East Sunset Way. Your stroll ends at Boehm's Candies, where you can pick up hand-dipped chocolates to carry you through the chilly afternoon.

Finding a place to park on Front Street North can test your patience when everyone is ducking into shops to escape the rain. Skip the endless loop around the block and head straight for the free public lots near City Hall on Rainier Blvd S or the Transit Center Park and Ride. If a sudden downpour hits while you are out, the indoor exhibits and covered observation bridges at the Issaquah Salmon Hatchery provide a dry spot to watch the swollen creek.

The commercial strip along the valley floor reflects a rich tapestry of modern cultures. Down on 116th Ave SE, Shaah & Sheeko serves up hot Somali tea and desserts that bring instant warmth on a cold afternoon. Between the local cafes, community library programs, and welcoming spaces, February in the valley offers a quiet, grounded sense of connection.

### **EP3**

March brings sudden sun squalls and rushing water as snowmelt turns the local creeks into roaring torrents. The valley transitioned from hop farms in the 1880s to deep coal extraction at Grand Ridge and Cougar Mountain, leaving behind a rugged industrial heritage. In 1977, local conservationist Harvey Manning coined the term Issaquah Alps to group these surrounding peaks, ensuring their forested slopes would remain wild and accessible for generations.

Capitalize on a sudden break in the clouds by grabbing street tacos from Xochi on NW Gilman Blvd. Walk over to the paved Rainier Trail and trace the old railway corridor into Olde Town. You can sit on a wooden bench at Confluence Park to eat your lunch while watching the high spring water churn through the gravel beds of Issaquah Creek.

Spring weather brings out more drivers, making the evening commute from four to six in the evening on Front Street South particularly slow. Smart locals bypass the valley floor entirely by using Highlands Dr NE for Eastside trips. When running weekly errands at Fred Meyer or picking up supplies on Gilman, sticking to mid-morning hours saves you from waiting through multiple traffic light cycles.

Before the heavy summer hiking crowds arrive, the lower mountain trails belong to those who know where to look. The Snoqualmie Tribe continues to guide habitat restoration along these waterways, protecting the delicate salmon ecosystems. Early morning walks along Tibbetts Creek or the shaded trailhead at Squak Mountain State Park offer a tranquil window into the valley's natural rhythms.

### **EP4**

April brings a visible shift across the valley as daylight stretches past seven in the evening and cherry blossoms open up. Local history sits right along the sidewalk at 232 Front Street North, where the Hailstone Feed Store was constructed in 1910. It served as a Shell gas station, auto repair shop, and farm supplier for over a century before being restored as the town visitor center.

Start an April morning by picking up a warm espresso from Winnies Cafe & Deli. Take your cup down the paved Rainier Trail toward Confluence Park, crossing the wide wooden footbridge over Issaquah Creek. The grassy meadows here are just beginning to green up, making it an ideal spot to sit outside and listen to the birds returning to the riparian trees.

Weekend afternoons bring plenty of visitors to Olde Town, causing street parking on Front Street North to fill up early. Your best bet is to leave your car in the public access lots along Rainier Blvd S or walk from the Transit Center lot. The flat terrain around the valley core makes it easy to leave the car behind when running simple errands between local shops.

Diversity thrives along the commercial corridors that thread through the lower valley. Food trucks like Little Sonora bring fantastic regional Mexican flavors to local parking lots, complementing the established cafes nearby. Between the inclusive arts programming at Village Theatre and open public parks, the community steps out into the fresh spring air with renewed energy.

### **EP5**

May heralds the return of long twilights and clear skies over the surrounding ridges. At 120 Front Street North, the Village Theatre First Stage building has stood since 1913, having originally served as a silent film hall and indoor basketball court. That legacy of community gathering continues today as local youth theater and performances keep the historic boards alive.

Plan a late afternoon outing by having dinner at Chan Kitchen on Front Street. Afterward, walk past the Memorial Field gazebo and drive a short distance down Issaquah-Hobart Road to Chirico Field. You can sit on the open grass and watch colorful paragliders drift silently down from the heights of Tiger Mountain against the evening sky.

If you plan to hike up to Poo Poo Point yourself, be warned that the Chirico Trailhead parking lot fills up completely before seven-thirty on weekend mornings. The local secret is to aim for a late afternoon hike arriving after four in the afternoon, which lets you catch the peak thermal winds and avoids the morning trail congestion.

As the weather warms, evening dog walks along the paved Rainier Trail become a neighborhood ritual. The ancestral roots of the Snoqualmie Indian Tribe remain present in the landscape, from the protected riverbanks to the steep timbered slopes. For those seeking absolute quiet away from the crowds, the peaceful trailheads at Squak Mountain offer a cool, shaded retreat.

### **EP6**

June arrives with solstice light that lingers well past nine at night, turning the valley into an open-air living room. Long before state lawmakers established the official city name in 1899, civil engineer Daniel H. Gilman brought the Seattle, Lake Shore and Eastern Railroad here in 1887. The tracks are long gone, but that historic transportation corridor still dictates how the town moves and gathers.

Grab a quick bite from Tacos El Bajio on a sunny June evening, then stroll over to Confluence Park to admire the community garden beds. From there, make your way over to the Memorial Field gazebo, where free outdoor summer concerts bring neighbors together on the grass as the sun dips behind Squak Mountain.

Summer traffic on Front Street South gets heavy during the afternoon rush from four to six in the evening. Drivers heading toward I-90 can avoid central town delays by turning onto Sunset Way or using 2nd Ave SE to slip past the main intersections. Planning major shopping trips along NW Gilman Blvd during off-peak morning hours keeps your day moving smoothly.

Early June brings a lively spirit to the neighborhood, complete with Pride displays throughout Olde Town storefronts and youth events at the local library. Down at Lake Sammamish State Park, paddleboarders take to the calm water, while the quiet forest trails on Tiger Mountain offer a shaded sanctuary when the afternoon heat hits the valley floor.

### **EP7**

July brings peak summer heat, dry dirt trails, and bright skies over the Issaquah Alps. The valley's past as a heavy coal mining center at Grand Ridge contrasts sharply with its modern status as the global headquarters for Costco Wholesale, which moved here in 1994. Yet despite major corporate presence, the town preserves its pioneer roots through strict historic design standards along Front Street.

On a warm July afternoon, grab a cold craft beer at Dubtown Brewing Company or Bickersons Brewhouse. Take a leisurely walk up toward the public bench overlooks along East Sunset Way, where you can sit under the shade trees and take in expansive views of Olde Town and the mountain slopes beyond.

Finding parking near popular summer spots like Lake Sammamish or Olde Town can be tough on eighty-degree days. The best trick is parking at the free public lots near the Community Center on Rainier Blvd S and walking the paved Rainier Trail to reach your destination. If you need to run quick errands at Target or Fred Meyer, going late in the evening avoids the hot asphalt and crowded lots.

Summer in the valley is deeply tied to the surrounding waters, known natively as *is-kwah-ah* for the sound of the birds that gather here. The Snoqualmie Tribe continues to advocate for the health of these waterways, reminding locals of the land's original stewards. For those needing a break from the sun, the quiet wetland boardwalks at Tradition Plateau offer a cool, sensory-rich escape.

### **EP8**

August carries a golden, hazy light that settles over the mountain ridges during late afternoon hours. Decades ago, Isaac Brock of Modest Mouse drew inspiration from these very hills and valleys, channeling the quiet isolation of the PNW into songs that reached far beyond King County. That atmospheric beauty is most obvious when the sun drops low over Tiger Mountain.

Start your late summer afternoon with a refreshing drink from CrossCo Coffee Company or a hearty meal at Gip's Down Home BBQ. Take a walk along the paved Rainier Trail as the shadows lengthen, then drive over to Chirico Field to watch paragliders touch down gently on the grass during the warm golden hour.

Commuter traffic along SR 900 and Front Street S remains dense during late summer afternoons. Local drivers know to use 2nd Ave SE as an effective local bypass when traveling across town. For big-box shopping runs, the commercial strip along NW Gilman Blvd offers easy access, though visiting during weekday mornings is always the stress-free choice.

The culinary scene along the valley floor keeps summer evenings lively with options like Las Bugambilias and Taqueria Birrieria Perla serving authentic Mexican fare. As the month winds down, watching the sunset from the launches at Poo Poo Point gives you an unbeatable view stretching across Lake Sammamish to the Bellevue skyline.

### **EP9**

September brings a crisp edge back to the morning air as autumn settles into the valley floor. Back in 1909, the arrival of the Northwest Milk Condensing Company, which later became Darigold, marked a shift from raw mining to dairy production. Today, that agricultural history gives way to seasonal ecological milestones that draw residents out to the water.

On a crisp September morning, grab a fresh brew from Issaquah Coffeeroasters in Olde Town. Walk down to the Issaquah Salmon Hatchery along Issaquah Creek, where you can stand on the wooden observation bridges and watch the very first returning Chinook and Coho salmon make their journey upstream to spawn.

With local schools back in session, morning traffic along Front Street South slows down considerably between six and nine in the morning. Cutting through Highlands Dr NE allows commuters to bypass the central Olde Town gridlock when heading toward regional highways. Sticking to bicycle or foot travel along the Rainier Trail makes local errands effortless.

The arrival of the salmon is a powerful annual ritual tied directly to the ancestral heritage of the Snoqualmie Indian Tribe, who lead watershed conservation efforts along the creek tributaries. For a quiet sensory walk on a brisk autumn morning, head to the shaded paths along Tibbetts Creek or explore the quiet entry trails at Squak Mountain State Park.

### **EP10**

October transforms the surrounding mountain slopes into a rich tapestry of yellow and gold fall foliage. Olde Town maintains strict historic preservation rules that keep pioneer storefront facades intact while enforcing critical riparian buffer rules along Issaquah Creek to protect the aquatic habitat running right through the center of town.

Treat yourself on a cool October morning with baked goods from Cookies In Bloom or Rosie Bakery. Take your treat over to Confluence Park and stroll along the creek paths, stopping at the hatchery observation decks to witness the peak salmon spawning run as hundreds of fish navigate the clear, rushing water.

Early October brings the massive Salmon Days Festival, which closes Front Street to vehicle traffic and brings over one hundred fifty thousand visitors to town. During festival weekend, driving through central Olde Town is impossible, so locals rely heavily on the Transit Center Park and Ride and walk into town via the Rainier Trail to bypass the crowds.

Autumn evenings call for cozy indoor stops and hearty soup across local cafes along NW Gilman Blvd. The damp forest trails around Tradition Plateau and the Swamp Trail offer a peaceful retreat where rain patters softly against the autumn leaves, creating a classic Pacific Northwest atmosphere.

### **EP11**

November brings the first serious winter storms, with steady rain drumming on metal roofs and clouds hanging low over the slopes. Environmental advocate Harvey Manning spent years fighting to protect these forested hills from sprawling development, ensuring that the wild green backdrop of the Issaquah Alps would always frame the modern town below.

On a dark, rainy afternoon, head over to Shaah & Sheeko for a steaming cup of spiced tea and sweet baked treats. Afterward, pop open your umbrella and take a slow stroll past the historic Hailstone Feed Store at 232 Front Street North to admire how gracefully the town's early twentieth-century architecture has aged.

Rain and early darkness at four-thirty in the afternoon tend to slow traffic along Front Street South to a crawl. Parking is easy to find in the free public lots near City Hall on Rainier Blvd S, allowing you to run quick errands on foot without dealing with wet, congested street parking on Front Street North.

The community gathers indoors during the dark November evenings, attending live theater and youth programs at the Village Theatre First Stage. For those who love the solitude of the Pacific Northwest winter, taking a rain-jacket walk along the quiet bench spots at Squak Mountain State Park offers the soothing sound of wind and rain through high evergreen branches.

### **EP12**

December wraps the valley in festive warmth, with holiday lights decorating the historic storefronts along Front Street. The Swiss legacy of Julius Boehm shines especially bright this month, as locals and visitors flock to East Sunset Way to visit his iconic chalet and the historic Swiss Chapel set against the dark winter backdrop of Tiger Mountain.

Enjoy an intimate December evening by having dinner at Montalcino Ristorante Italiano in Olde Town. Walk along the illuminated sidewalk of Front Street to take in the holiday lights, then finish your evening at Boehm's Candies to stock up on handcrafted holiday chocolates and European-style confections.

Holiday shopping rushes can create long lines near major retail hubs like Costco and Target along NW Gilman Blvd. Local residents dodge the parking lot madness by taking the paved Rainier Trail on foot or bicycle to complete quick errands in the commercial core without getting stuck in traffic.

The year ends as it began, grounded in the quiet majesty of the valley and its Coast Salish roots, where *is-kwah-ah* still evokes the winter call of birds along the creek. Whether you are browsing for local gifts in Olde Town or taking a quiet morning walk beside the rushing water at Confluence Park, December in the valley feels deeply welcoming.
